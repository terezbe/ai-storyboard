A letter from Rosa

Amore, read this first.

Giulia says a book needs a letter at the front. So: I talk, she types. If you find a mistake, it’s hers.

I know your mornings. The telephone is in your hand before your eyes are open. The coffee goes down standing at the sink. The keys are lost. Breakfast is nothing, or something in the car. And by nine o’clock you feel old. Older than me, maybe.

Everybody thinks their mornings are the worst in the world. Everybody is wrong. My Totò had three children under five and one bathroom. I have seen bad mornings, amore. Yours are ordinary, and that’s good news, because ordinary trouble has ordinary answers.

People see me come out of the sea at ninety-four and they say: lucky woman. Lucky genes, lucky sea. Ascolta. The sea is not my secret. The sea is my receipt. Eighty-five years of small mornings paid for it: light first, a big glass of water, the stairs, breakfast sitting down, lunch at one, a rest, a walk, people. Not one of those needs a sea. You can do every one of them in an apartment in a city.

So here’s the deal. Thirty mornings, one small thing each, so small you’ll feel a little silly. Do it, tick the box, write one line. That’s all. You won’t get younger; nobody can sell you that. But your mornings will feel like yours again, you’ll know which small things did it, and nine o’clock will stop feeling like the end of the day.

And you don’t have to swim. Please don’t. The sea is my story. Yours is a window, a glass of water and a chair.

Piano piano.

“The sea doesn’t care how old you are.”
Rosa

Before you start

Why this works

Giulia studies in the city and likes reasons, so she looked things up. I told her what I know. Here’s both.

Small and daily beats big and brave

Research on habits keeps finding that tiny actions tied to something you already do (waking up, the kettle, lunch) last longer than big plans that run on willpower. One well-known study found new habits took around two months on average to feel automatic, and much longer for some people. So thirty days starts the job; it doesn’t finish it. That’s why the book ends with you choosing five to keep.

“I didn’t need a study. I had a moka and a church bell.”

Light first

Sleep researchers keep finding that daylight early in the day helps set the body’s internal clock. That’s one reason people often feel more awake after morning light, and sleepier at the right time at night. Week one is built on this.

“I don’t know about clocks inside the body. I know about windows.”

The old Mediterranean pattern

Rosa’s town isn’t one of the famous Blue Zones, the handful of places where researchers found unusual numbers of people living into their nineties and past a hundred. One of them, Sardinia, is just across the water. What keeps coming up in those places is ordinary: walking every day because the town makes you, simple food built on beans, vegetables, bread and olive oil, meals eaten with other people, time to slow down, and a reason to get up. The traditional Mediterranean way of eating has been studied for decades and is often linked with healthier aging. Researchers still argue about exactly why, and they call these patterns, not promises.

“So do I.”

People count

Studies that follow people over many years keep finding that regular contact with others goes along with aging well. That’s week four.

“Concetta has been annoying me for sixty years. It’s good for both of us.”

What this book isn’t

It’s a set of everyday habits, not medical advice. It doesn’t treat or cure anything, and it can’t replace a doctor who knows you. If you’re tired every day no matter what you change, that’s for a doctor, not a nonna. As Rosa says: ask your doctor, then go outside.

When you miss a morning

Salvatore’s Rule

No restarts. No catching up. No doubling.

Salvatore fished all his life. Some mornings the sea said no, and the boats stayed in the harbor. Did he go out twice the next day to make up for it? Of course not. He’d have sunk the boat. He went out once, like always, and in his little book he wrote one word for the day he missed: storm. Then he forgot about it.

You will miss mornings, amore. Children get sick. Some mornings you sleep through everything. When it happens:

  • Don’t go back to Day 1. You haven’t lost anything.
  • Don’t do two pages tomorrow to catch up.
  • Don’t punish yourself with extra.
  • Open the book at the next page and do that one. Just that one.
From Salvatore’s little book

A day in this book is a morning you did, not a date on the calendar. If your thirty mornings take thirty-six days, they are still thirty mornings. Miss a whole week? Same rule. The next page is waiting, and it doesn’t ask where you were.

Before you start

Make it fit your life

If your life doesn’t fit the book, change the book, not your life.

These mornings are written for an ordinary day: a bed, a window, a kitchen, a start somewhere between six and nine. If that isn’t you, here’s how to bend it.

If you work shifts

Your morning is whenever you wake up. Salvatore’s started at three, in the dark. If you wake in the dark, switch on the brightest light for the Window Rule and get outside when the daylight comes. Pick one bell for work days and one for days off, as close together as your schedule allows.

If you have small children

My Totò had three under five. Do the rules with them: children love a rule with a name. A two-minute version counts. A version where somebody is crying counts too.

If your body has limits

Every movement here is gentle, and every one can be done slower, sitting down, or skipped. Go at your own pace. If you have a heart, lung, joint or balance condition, are pregnant, or have been told to take it easy, check with your doctor before week two. Pain means stop, not push. And if a doctor has told you how to eat, drink or move, the doctor wins.

If you live alone

Since Salvatore, my house is quiet. Quiet, not empty. Week four asks you to talk to people: a neighbor, the baker, a voice on the telephone. You choose the people. They don’t have to live with you.

When it’s more than mornings

If tiredness never lifts whatever you change, if you snore loudly or wake up gasping, or if your mood is low most days, see a doctor. That’s the strong move, not a failure. If you’re in a dark place or thinking about hurting yourself, call or text 988 in the US (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), free, any hour. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans on 116 123, free. Anywhere else, call your local emergency number.

Week One · Light and waterDay 1 of 30

The Window Rule

Before the telephone, the window. Wide open.

The first thing I touch every morning is the window. Not the moka, not the telephone: the window. I open it wide, both sides, all the way, and I stand there with the light on my face and look. The sea tells me what kind of day it is. Flat. Angry. Or that silver color it goes in February, when it’s thinking about something.

Giulia’s telephone sleeps next to her pillow like a cat. The first thing she sees every morning is a little glass box full of other people’s news. Then she comes into my kitchen with a face like yesterday’s bread and asks me why she’s tired.

Listen to me. Your eyes wake up before the rest of you. Give them the sky first, not the box. One minute. You don’t need a sea, amore. You don’t need anything, just a window. A parking lot has a sky over it too.

Today
Before you touch the telephone, go to a window and open it wide, not a crack. Stand there for one whole minute with the light on your face, looking at whatever is outside.
Too much today? If it’s dark or the window won’t open, pull the curtains wide and switch on the brightest light in the room. Then the telephone.

The Big Glass Rule

A big glass of water before the coffee. Not a little one.

Before the moka, a glass of water. A big one, amore. Not one of those little glasses you could hide in your fist. A real glass, full to the top.

My mother did it, and her mother, and nobody ever told them why. They just knew that a body that has been all night without a drink is like the hill behind the church in August. It doesn’t need a speech. It needs water.

I keep my glass upside down on the little table by the bed, so the dust stays out. In the morning I turn it over, fill it at the kitchen sink and drink it slowly, looking out of the window I just opened. Then I light the gas under the moka. Not before.

I’m not telling you water is magic. Giulia would make a face. I’m telling you it’s the easiest win of the whole day, and a day likes to start with a win.

Today
Before your coffee or tea, drink one big glass of water, full to the top. Tonight, put the glass out so it’s waiting for you.
Too much today? Drink half the big glass before the coffee and the other half after it.

The Wrist Rule

Cool water on the face and the wrists.

You want to know about the sea. Everybody does. Yes: every morning since I was nine, down the steps, down the ladder, in. Eighty-five years. Only when the sea is kind, and never alone: there is always somebody on the rocks. On rough days I stay by the ladder and splash, and I’m not ashamed to say so.

But I’m telling you this so you won’t do it. The sea is my story, amore, not your homework. What you want isn’t the sea. It’s the moment: the first cool touch that says, awake now.

So. The kitchen sink. Cool water on the face, three times. Then on the inside of the wrists. My little anchor gets wet every morning. I got it at seventeen, on a dare from a sailor’s sister, and my mother didn’t speak to me for a month. Worth it.

Today
Splash cool tap water on your face three times, then let it run over the insides of your wrists for a few seconds. Cool, not icy.
Too much today? Hold a cool, wet washcloth against your face for ten seconds.

The Doorstep Rule

Outside in the first hour, even for five minutes.

My stool lives by the front door. Low, wooden, with one leg that wobbles if you sit on it wrong. In the morning, before the town is properly awake, I sit there between my geraniums and my basil and watch the alley wake up. The baker’s boy going down. The cats going up. The laundry on the line, deciding whether to dry.

Five minutes. I’m not doing anything, and that’s the point. I’m outside, in the real light. Not the light from a screen, not the light through glass. Giulia read me something that says even a cloudy morning outside is much brighter than a lamp indoors. I said: I know, amore. I have eyes.

You have a doorstep too, or a balcony. If you have neither, you have the walk to the car. Take it slowly and look up.

Today
Get outside within an hour of waking, for at least five minutes: the doorstep, the balcony, or the slow way to the car.
Too much today? Stand in your open front door for one minute and look at the sky.

The Basil Rule

Give something alive a drink.

Every morning, after my own glass, I water the pots by the door. Two of basil, three of geraniums, and one of something Concetta gave me in 1998 that has never flowered once. I water that one too. Maybe this year.

People think the plants need me. Ha. I need the plants. They’re the reason I open the door at seven instead of sitting at the table looking at nothing. Something alive is waiting out there, and it’s thirsty, and it doesn’t care how I slept.

So give something alive a drink this morning: a pot on the windowsill, or the cheap herbs from the market standing in a jar of water. It takes one minute, and it puts you at the window, in the light, before the day starts shouting at you.

Today
Water a plant this morning. No plant? Today, put a cheap pot of herbs, or a green cutting in a jar of water, on your brightest windowsill.
Too much today? Put any green stem in a glass of water and stand it by the window you opened on Day 1.

The Hallway Rule

The telephone sleeps outside the bedroom.

When I was a bride, there was one telephone in this town, in the bar on the square. If somebody called from America, a boy ran up the steps to fetch you, and the whole bar listened. Later we had our own, black and heavy, on a little table in the hall. It rang, you walked to it. It never once came to bed with us.

You remember Giulia’s telephone, sleeping by her pillow like a cat? One weekend she forgot it in my kitchen all night. In the morning she said, Nonna, I slept like a stone. I said: I know, amore. The cat was in the kitchen.

So tonight your telephone sleeps outside the bedroom. If it’s your alarm, put it across the room, so you have to stand up to make it stop. Standing up is half the morning. The other half is the window, and you already know that one.

Today
Tonight, charge your telephone outside the bedroom. If it’s your alarm, put it across the room, where you have to get up to turn it off.
Too much today? Leave it in the room tonight, but face down and out of arm’s reach.

The Bell Rule

One time to get up, every day. Sunday too.

I have never owned a watch. I don’t need one. The church bell rings, the boats come in, and I know what time it is. The bell rings at seven every morning: Monday, Thursday, Christmas, it doesn’t care. It has never once slept in.

Your body likes a bell too, amore. You get up at six for work and at eleven on Saturday, and then on Monday morning you feel like you crossed the ocean in the night. That isn’t age. That’s a clock you keep moving around.

So pick your bell. One time to get up, every day, weekends too, within half an hour. On Sunday I put on my navy dress with the white dots, and I still get up with the bell. After that I can be as lazy as I like. Lazy after the bell is allowed. Lazy instead of the bell is how a week gets ruined.

Today
Choose one wake-up time for every day this week, weekends included, within half an hour either way. Set it tonight.
Too much today? Keep your weekend wake-up within an hour of your weekday one.

The Counting Rule

Take one flight of stairs on purpose, and count it.

A hundred and twelve steps from the sea to my door. I know because I count them. Out loud, some mornings, and the neighbors count along to tease me. Let them. Counting keeps the mind busy, so it has no time to complain.

There is no elevator in my town. There isn’t one in my house, there isn’t one in my life, and I don’t miss it. An elevator is a little box where people stand too close together and look at the ceiling. Steps are better company.

You don’t need a hundred and twelve, amore. Not today. Maybe not ever. You need your own stairs, taken on purpose instead of by accident. One flight. A hand on the rail, always, and especially on the way down. Count them. Then write the number down below, so you know it.

Today
Take one flight of stairs on purpose today instead of the elevator or the escalator, with a hand on the rail. Count the steps.
Too much today? No stairs, or stairs are hard for you? Walk to the end of the hallway and back, counting your steps.

The Cat Rule

Stretch before you stand.

The cats on my steps know something. Watch one wake up. It never jumps up and runs. It stretches: front legs, back legs, a big yawn with all its teeth. Then it walks off like it owns the town, which it does.

People do the opposite. The alarm screams, they jump out of bed like the house is on fire, and then they wonder why their back grumbles all morning.

So before your feet touch the floor, be a cat for one minute. Arms over your head, long, like you’re reaching for the top shelf where I hide the good cookies from Giulia. Point your toes, then pull them back. Circle your ankles. Then roll onto your side, push yourself up slowly with your arms, and sit on the edge of the bed for three breaths. Then stand. Then the window.

Today
Before you get out of bed, stretch slowly for one minute: arms up, toes pointed then pulled back, ankles circled. Sit on the edge of the bed for three breaths before you stand.
Too much today? Skip the stretch and just sit on the edge of the bed for three slow breaths before you stand.

The Bread Rule

Walk to the shop for something small. No car, no delivery.

Every morning I go down for bread. One loaf. I could buy two and keep one, Giulia says, very clever. No. The going is the point. Down the alley, past the church, down to the baker. Twenty minutes there and back, if nobody stops me. Somebody always stops me.

Giulia showed me pictures of the big suburbs, all the houses the same. Madonna. Everything is a car ride from everything, and now people don’t even drive: a boy on a bicycle brings dinner to the door. You can live a whole week and never walk further than from the sofa to the door.

So today you go yourself. Something small: bread, milk, a lemon, a newspaper. On your own feet, not in the car, and not on the telephone. Nothing heavy to carry home. Just the small thing, and some air.

Today
Walk to a shop or bakery yourself today for something small, instead of driving or ordering it.
Too much today? If every shop is too far to walk, drive, park at the far end of the lot, and walk the rest of the way.

The Tall Rule

Stand like you’re watching for the boats.

When the boats were late, the wives stood on the harbor wall and watched the water. Nobody slouched. You don’t slouch when you’re looking for your husband’s boat. You stand tall, chin level, eyes far out. I stood like that for years, and now my back does it by itself.

Giulia sits like a question mark. Over the telephone, over the computer, over her coffee. She’s twenty and she’s bent over like an old man mending nets.

Here is my mother’s trick. Stand with your back against a wall: heels, bottom, shoulders and the back of your head, all touching it. Not pushing. Just touching. Thirty seconds. That’s what tall feels like. Then step away from the wall and try to keep it, like you’re carrying a full glass across the room.

Today
Three times today, stand with your back against a wall for thirty seconds: heels, bottom, shoulders and head gently touching.
Too much today? Once is enough. If your head doesn’t reach the wall comfortably, let it be. Never force it.

The Heron Rule

While the coffee makes itself, practice your balance.

In winter a heron comes to the rocks. Gray, serious, like a priest. It stands on one leg for an hour and never falls in. I don’t have an hour. I have the moka.

The moka takes four or five minutes to start singing. That’s a long time to stare at a pot. So I hold the edge of the counter with both hands and lift one foot, just a little, off the floor. I count to ten. Then the other foot. Some mornings I hold on with only one hand, and some mornings I don’t. Both are fine.

Balance is like the dialect my mother spoke. I still have it because I use it every day. Keep both hands on the counter until you’re sure of yourself. There’s no prize for falling, amore. Not in my kitchen.

Today
While your coffee or tea is brewing, hold the counter with both hands and lift one foot just off the floor for a count of ten. Then the other foot.
Too much today? Do it sitting down: lift one foot a little off the floor, then the other.

The Stool Rule

Stand up slowly, and let your legs do the work.

Concetta uses the wall to get up from her chair. I see her do it. I don’t say anything. (I say it to everybody else.)

When I get up from my stool by the door, I put my feet flat, lean forward until my nose is over my toes, and stand up with my legs. Then I sit down again slowly, as if there’s an egg on the stool and I don’t want to break it. Five times, while the alley watches. Concetta pretends not to look.

Getting up from a chair is something you do every day of your life, so practice it, amore. Not fast. Not proud. A sturdy chair with no wheels, its back against the wall so it can’t slide. Use your hands on the armrests if you need them. That isn’t cheating. That’s sense.

Today
Sit in a sturdy chair with no wheels. Five times, slowly, stand up and sit back down. Use your hands on the armrests if you need to.
Too much today? Do it twice, or just once, slowly, with all the help you need.

The Broom Rule

One job by hand, done properly.

I ran my house like a boat. Everything has its place, everything gets done, and nobody waits for somebody else to do it. On a boat there’s no machine for anything. You do it with your hands.

So every morning I sweep. The kitchen, then my step, then a bit of the alley that isn’t mine, because if I don’t, who will? Ten minutes with the broom and my arms and my back have said good morning. Then I hang the laundry on the line across the alley, arms up, clothespins in my mouth like my mother.

You have machines for everything. Good. Keep them. But pick one job today and give it to your body. Sweep a floor, or carry the groceries in from the car with one bag in each hand, like a sensible person. Slowly. Properly. Then stand back and look at it, done.

Today
Do one household job by hand today, slowly and properly: sweep a floor, hang laundry, or carry the groceries in with one bag in each hand.
Too much today? Five minutes tidying one table or counter, standing up.

A letter for Day 15

Halfway, amore.

Giulia, write this exactly the way I say it.

You’re halfway. Fourteen mornings. And if it took you twenty days to get here, it’s still fourteen mornings. I don’t count the storms.

Now I’ll tell you something nobody told me when I was young. This is the place. Right here. Not Day 1, when everything is new and you buy a nice pen. Not Day 29, when you can see the end. Here, in the middle, when it isn’t new anymore and the end is still far. This is where people put the book in a drawer.

When I swim, there’s a moment a little way out, with the ladder behind me and the white rock still ahead, when a small voice says: why are you doing this? Go home. Have your coffee. Eighty-five years, and that voice still comes some mornings. (Never alone and never in a rough sea, I promise you. Somebody is always on the rocks.) I don’t argue with it. I don’t win by being strong. I just take the next stroke. Only the next one. Then the voice gets bored and goes away.

That moment is the whole game. This exact moment. So don’t think about sixteen more mornings. Think about tomorrow’s page. It’s small. They’re all small. I made them small on purpose.

Before you turn the page, go back and read what you wrote under “What I noticed.” All of it, even the lazy lines. That’s fourteen mornings you didn’t have before.

Monday LentilsOne pot, three days
Better on Wednesday. Don’t ask me why. (The One Pot Rule, page 31.)
You need
  • 1 cup brown or green lentils, rinsed
  • 1 onion, 1 carrot and 1 celery stalk, chopped small
  • 1 clove garlic, sliced, and 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 cup canned crushed tomatoes, 1 bay leaf
  • About 4 cups water, and salt
Do this
  1. Warm the oil and cook the onion, carrot and celery gently for 8 to 10 minutes, until soft.
  2. Add the garlic for one minute.
  3. Add the lentils, tomatoes, bay leaf and water. Bring to a boil, then turn it down to a gentle simmer.
  4. Cook 30 to 40 minutes, until the lentils are soft. Add a little water if it gets too thick.
  5. Salt at the end. Take out the bay leaf. A little olive oil on top in the bowl.
Keep it. Let it cool, cover it, keep it in the fridge, and eat it within three days.

Caponata (Not Concetta’s)Sweet and sour, better tomorrow
Concetta puts in too much sugar. I’m not saying this to be unkind. I’m saying it because it’s true. She fries the eggplant. I roast it. I’m right.
You need
  • 1 large eggplant, in cubes
  • 1 onion, chopped, and 2 celery stalks, sliced
  • 4 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 cup canned crushed tomatoes
  • 2 tablespoons capers, rinsed, and a handful of pitted green olives
  • 3 tablespoons red wine vinegar, 1 tablespoon sugar (one, Concetta), salt, fresh basil
Do this
  1. Heat the oven to 425°F (220°C). Toss the eggplant with half the oil and a pinch of salt and roast 25 to 30 minutes, until soft and golden.
  2. Meanwhile cook the onion and celery gently in the rest of the oil for 10 minutes.
  3. Add the tomatoes, capers and olives, and simmer 10 minutes.
  4. Stir in the eggplant, vinegar and sugar and simmer 5 minutes more. Taste: sweet and sour together.
  5. Rest it, until tomorrow if you can. Basil on top. Eat it at room temperature, with bread.
Keep it. Covered in the fridge, up to four days.

The honest small print

Ascolta. This part matters, so I’ll say it plainly.

I’m not a real person.

Nonna Rosa is a fictional character created with AI. So are Giulia, Concetta, Salvatore, the town and the 112 steps. The habits in this book are real and ordinary: daylight, water, walking, sitting down to eat, rest and people. They have been part of everyday life in many places for a very long time. You don’t need me to believe in them.

This is not medical advice.

It’s general information about everyday habits. It doesn’t diagnose, treat or cure anything, it isn’t therapy, and it doesn’t replace a doctor who knows you. If a doctor has told you how to eat, drink, move or rest, follow the doctor.

Move gently.

Go at your own pace. Stop if anything hurts or makes you dizzy or short of breath. If you have a condition, are pregnant or are recovering from something, ask your doctor before the movement week.

Never the sea.

Nothing in this book asks you to swim, or to get into cold or open water. The sea is my story, not your homework.

Food.

The kitchen cards are ordinary home recipes. Mind your allergies, cook fish all the way through, and keep leftovers in the fridge.

When it’s more than mornings.

Tiredness that never lifts, low mood most days, or weeks of bad sleep: see a doctor.
